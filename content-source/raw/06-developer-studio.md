**Subtitle:** Designing a brand new way for clients to access Nielsen's vast collection of data & insights.

## Some background on Nielsen & Developer Studio.

Nielsen is the world leader in market research data. They collect data on everything from what people watch on TV, browse on their phones, & buy at the store. With all that data comes great responsibility.

Traditionally, Nielsen has provided high-touch services to large organizations for their data needs. This service model involves a lot of manual processes & teams to operate.

So in 2015, Nielsen embarked on a new mission to unlock its massive collection of data and provide a modern platform for clients to access data on their own terms. No more unnecessary gatekeeping, processes, or costs passed on to the client.

A key part of this vision was a tool called Nielsen Developer Studio. This solution would allow technical users to:

- Search across Nielsen's data warehouse to find the right datasets for their needs.
- Narrow down the dataset so they only pay for what they need.
- Choose the data format that works best for them (API, Extract, or Virtual Cube).
- Place their order & begin using their data.

## My Process in 7 Steps.

### 1. Understand who would be using our product

Based on initial research, we discovered there are two key personas who would be using Developer Studio most often:

**Developers:**
- Highly technical people who want to quickly get the data they need & get on with their work.
- Usually getting their data through an API so they can plug it into their app or BI tool.

**Analysts:**
- Data-minded people who want to explore the different types of Nielsen data available & make an informed decision.
- Usually receiving their data via an Extract which they analyze and process via their own methods.

### 2. Brainstorm solutions

I began by creating a Journey Map to help the team understand the ideal journey of our soon-to-be users.

I then worked through several different process flows that illustrated more technical facets of the journey.

Finally, I led a Sketchy Session with the entire team so we could source ideas from everyone & make sure their voices were heard and understood.

Sketchy Sessions are my favorite brainstorming technique because they're casual (so there's no pressure) and involves everyone in the design process which leads to great ideas and makes everyone feel involved from the get go.

### 3. Create mid-fidelity prototypes

Using the ideas generated during the brainstorming phase, I began building them in more detail & iterating using Axure.

**Landing Page Options** — This would become the public facing landing page so it was paramount to make a captivating first impression.

**Dashboard** — This is where users would spend most of their time so it needed to be simple yet effective in allowing users to carry out their tasks.

### 4. Conduct first round of usability testing

Once I had a basic end to end flow of the experience built via a prototype, it was time to test the concept with users. I facilitated this research study by doing the following:

- Framed the outline of key moments & wrote the test script.
- Created a survey to capture quantitative data at the end of each test.
- Recruited participants that aligned with our main personas (developers & analysts).
- Had the design intern I was mentoring capture notes as I led the sessions.
- Facilitated 10 usability sessions remotely.
- Compiled and synthesized the test results with our design intern.
- Presented the results to the entire product team.
- Shared my point of view for areas of improvements and sourced ideas from the team.

### 5. Iterate based on research findings

After the research study wrapped up and I had shared the results with the team, I got to work on iterating based on user feedback. I worked in collaboration with the product manager and a visual designer during this phase.

Key improvements:

- Consolidated all Apps (personal & org-level) into a single pane for a better use of space and to prevent confusion.
- Created a more generous area for users to quickly browse popular data sets and updated the name from "My Access" to "Data Access" to be more clear what that pane is for.
- Moved the security panel so it wasn't so prominent since users only need that information on occasion.
- Added a progress bar to the data search flow to better establish user expectations and make the process clear.

### 6. Test latest design & create high-fidelity prototype

I followed a similar process to the first round of research but used my latest designs. The results this time around were much better from both a qualitative & quantitative standpoint:

- Users were successful in completing various tasks without needing assistance.
- Achieved a high Experience Measurement score (one of the top scores among Nielsen products).

Based on this round of testing, I made a few refinements and focused my effort on bringing the designs to high-fidelity.

### 7. Work with Engineering to implement

This phase was actually happening throughout this entire design process. I kept in lockstep with the team as I designed to make sure they were building in a scalable way with minimal rework based on design updates.

I throughly enjoy working with engineers to bring designs to life. Some of the ways I supported them were:

- Creation of detailed specs for every screen & state.
- Writing detailed user stories for them to work towards.
- Working with the team to solve technical challenges as they arose.

## Reflecting on this project.

This was my first true test as a Product Designer. I was responsible for leading all aspects of Developer Studio's design from beginning to end. Thanks to the support of the broader design team, I successfully designed & launched one of Nielsen's most important strategic projects and shifted the paradigm of how clients access & use their data.

I learned a ton from this experience. A few key lessons learned were:

- The importance of always advocating for the end user, even if that means pushing back against engineering constraints in favor of a better experience.
- Working as a partner with Product Management and communicating design decisions clearly.
- Knowing when to ask for help & feedback from the broader design team.
- When in doubt, rely on user research to help light the way forward.
