## Context

<LexioContext />

I joined as the 102nd employee. Our small design team of three consisted of myself as Senior
Designer, a Director and a Junior Designer. The company was very engineering-led — it was
founded in 2010 by some of the leading AI engineers of the time — so as a design team we
often co-created with our engineering colleagues.

### The problem with the original story experience

The stories in Lexio were captivating and a real achievement for the company, but something
was missing.

On the right side was an area for users to "explore" — and it functioned pretty similarly to
the dashboard experience we were trying to replace. Users still had to know what questions to
ask, then use dropdowns to look at different breakdowns and comparisons.

That worked well enough for analytical users. But this product was built for people who
aren't data savvy, like executives. How would they know what selections to make?

## Reframe

The explore panel had quietly recreated the thing Lexio existed to replace. We had automated
the *answer* while still requiring the user to supply the *question* — which is precisely the
step a non-analyst can't take.

So Follow-Up Stories began as a strategic initiative to reinforce Lexio's core intellectual
property as an intelligent data storytelling product. We set out to:

- Let Lexio **recommend other stories** based on what the user is reading
- Give users a way to **dig deeper** and read more about something Lexio wrote
- Establish a **trail of record** from what the user reads, so recommendations get smarter

The key value: empowering and guiding *all* people to explore and understand their data, not
just analysts who already know what to ask.

The central design challenge was **balancing intelligent automation with user trust and
control.**

## Process

### Understanding the system before designing for it

The Follow-Up Stories engine had been in development for a couple of months before I began on
the experience. Before sketching anything, I needed a deep understanding of what this complex
system could actually do.

I teamed up with our Chief Scientist and worked with him daily to establish a framework the
whole team could understand, not just the engineers. It took the form of a visual **map**
showing how certain stories and their components — measures, timeframes — could lead to
others.

That map aligned every stakeholder from product to engineering. Before it existed, our teams
were talking in circles about how the system should work.

### Sketching the interaction model

With the conceptual model agreed, I explored several directions:

- Recommended stories in a **modal vs. a sidebar**
- Showing story **titles only vs. preview content**
- Tracking a user's trail via **breadcrumbs vs. tabs**
- How users might share stories and collaborate

I aligned on using the right-side area to surface recommended stories, with a tab system up
top for navigating between them. I brought that into Sketch and worked through UI options
using real story content — a fast iterative process, several layouts a day, hundreds of
screens by the end. I stitched screens together in InVision and moved to research.

### What research revealed

Sessions with six Lexio users testing a basic flow surfaced:

- Users **liked the overall layout** — main story left, Follow-Up Stories right
- But they were **hesitant to click Read More**, unsure what would happen
- The tab system **wasn't easily discoverable**; once noticed it was intuitive and reminded
  them of web browsers
- Some stories had **too many clickable phrases**, which felt overwhelming
- The story types — Track, Breakdown, Compare, Drivers — were welcomed and understood

## Craft

Changes made directly from those findings:

- An affordance to **expand** a Follow-Up Story and preview its content before committing to
  it — answering the "what will happen if I click" hesitation
- **Redesigned tabs** with greater visibility and descriptive labels
- **Fewer underlined phrases**, to clean up the interface and improve readability
- A descriptive label — **"What to read next"** — above the recommendations
- New **story type icons**, made with our illustrator, communicating each type's purpose and
  cohesive with Lexio's brand

### The trust problem I wasn't looking for

While testing the usability of the recommendation system, I uncovered a tangential insight.
When users read a phrase like *"bookings (sales) are $65k"*, it's important that they trust
that number — and the old experience gave them no way to check. I noticed that these same
users, working in Lexio in production, would sometimes flip back to the original data source
like Salesforce to double-check what Lexio had written.

That's where the concept of **inspecting** came from. A simple underline indicated when a
phrase could be clicked to reveal the actual numbers and data behind it in a modal.

It quickly became one of the most common interactions in Lexio stories, and gave users
comfort, confidence and trust in what the AI was writing.

### Implementation

We operated dual-track. I was in constant contact with engineering as I designed, so they
could build in lockstep, and I was explicit about which features were more settled than
others so they knew where to focus and avoid reworking the UI later. The new experience
launched within about a month of my being design complete.

## Outcome

- **94% increase** in average session duration
- The **inspect** interaction became one of the most common in Lexio stories
- 7 months start to finish, 6 users interviewed, 279 Sketch screens

## Reflection

Follow-Up Stories was easily the most daunting and arduous project I've worked on, for a
combination of reasons: it's powered by an incredibly complex AI system, there were very few
direct comparisons in the market to lean on, there was a lot of back and forth between
stakeholders on how the system should behave, and the majority of the work happened at the
onset of the pandemic.

I overcame those by teaming up with the engineering team to create a visual map everyone
could comprehend, taking "micro-inspiration" from a variety of products and services, getting
stakeholder alignment through design iteration, and scheduling frequent check-ins with the
broader team to validate my approach.

**What I would do differently:**

- **Push for the visual diagram of the system first**, before the low-fidelity UI. We spent a
  lot of time early on discussing the system's logic through designs, when a diagram would
  have been easier — it was closer to the truth.
- **Get involved with engineering earlier**, even just listening in. I had to play catch-up
  quickly, since the teams had been discussing the system for some time before I started.
