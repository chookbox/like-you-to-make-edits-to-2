## Context

An historic icon in the industrial supply sector, McMaster-Carr has been in business for over
120 years and offers around 800,000 products that help manufacturers, engineers and students
innovate.

While many know McMaster for their hefty yellow print catalogue, most of their business now
comes via the website and mobile app. With **over 400,000 visits per day**, McMaster.com is
heavily used by a range of customers searching for complex and technical items. Every design
decision, no matter how small, has a massive impact.

Comparison is a key behavior for customers before making a final decision to purchase.
Without a dedicated comparison experience, McMaster customers were forced into their own
workarounds.

By digging through McMaster's customer feedback history and querying a graph database that
records user interactions, I found customers were scrolling back and forth across the product
tables, opening individual products in separate browser tabs, or even **adding items to their
cart just to compare them**. Those methods work, but there's a lot of friction and they aren't
efficient.

At the direction of my manager I set out to design a comparison experience that would relieve
that pain and give customers more confidence in their selections.

## Reframe

One principle shaped everything: **McMaster customers are very resistant to change**, so it's
important to align designs to existing customer behavior wherever possible.

That rules out the obvious move. The standard answer is a dedicated compare page — select
items, go somewhere else, view a table. But at a site with 400,000 daily visits from people
doing technical work under time pressure, the risk isn't a mediocre comparison table. It's
disrupting a behavior customers have already built their working habits around.

So the problem wasn't "McMaster is missing a comparison feature." It was "**customers are
already comparing — badly — and the friction is in their workarounds.**"

That reframing changes what a good solution looks like. It's why the concept that won was the
one that never took the customer anywhere. And it's why unblocking a keyboard shortcut counted
as a real result rather than a consolation prize.

## Process

### Setting up the foundation

Before designing anything I created a project plan to give myself structure and set
expectations with the leadership team, laying out activities following the double diamond
along with time estimates for each to hold myself accountable.

Then I dug into the problem space, ran initial quantitative research on customer behavior,
and gathered third-party inspiration.

### A quick win, found in the data

The interaction data turned up something immediate: **our own site was blocking CTRL+Click**,
the shortcut for opening links in new tabs — one of the primary ways customers were comparing
products. I went straight to the engineering team to enable it while I worked on more robust
solutions.

### Diverging

To force breadth early, I sketched along a spectrum, from minimal lightweight solutions to
ideas closer to traditional e-commerce comparison experiences. After iterating and sharing
mid-fidelity mockups with my manager and the design team, I took forward two ideas from
**opposite ends** of that spectrum:

- **Pinned Rows** — comparison in place, nothing to navigate to
- **Compare Product Details** — a dedicated, fuller comparison view

### Testing both

I built interactive prototypes of each in Figma and ran a study with **five current customers
who had previously contacted us** asking to compare more easily on McMaster.com. I asked
discovery questions about how they use the site today, then had them complete tasks in each
prototype.

I used research best practice to keep the test unbiased — swapping the order the prototypes
were shown in, for instance — and followed up with a survey to capture quantitative data
supporting the qualitative insights.

One participant, a senior designer at an engineering firm, **prints pages off the site** to
compare details side by side on his desk.

### What we learned

- Participants usually only compare when looking at **unfamiliar products**, and mainly
  compare similar products within the same category
- **Four of five preferred Pinned Rows** for its lightweight nature — it didn't require them
  to navigate somewhere else
- Those same four suggested **both concepts could be combined** for the best of both worlds

## Craft

The customers resolved the either/or I'd set up, so the final design took both:

- **Combined the approaches into a unified prototype.** Pin products to compare in place; if
  further comparison is needed after pinning, a link takes you to the Compare Product Details
  page.
- **Discoverability fixes from testing** — animating the Pinned Items header as it appears
  and giving it more visual emphasis, so it gets noticed.
- **Designed for what was coming next**, considering how this would work alongside
  Collections, a way for customers to save products for later.

### Making the case to leadership

Using Miro, I presented to the leadership team — VPs from design, engineering and product —
to argue why a comparison experience would be valuable for McMaster customers:

- Background and context: problem statement, evidence, project scope
- Both design concepts, overlaid with customer feedback from research
- The latest iterations, and how the experience fits the broader ecosystem of features

## Outcome

**To be straight about where this landed: the comparison experience was validated and
approved, not launched.** Following the review, leadership added the project to the roadmap to
be piloted on the website and tested before any broader release. I left McMaster-Carr before
that pilot ran, so there are no post-launch numbers to report.

What did ship, and what the work produced:

- **CTRL+Click unblocked across the site** — a small fix, but it removed friction from a
  comparison behavior customers were already relying on every day
- A design **validated with the customers who had asked for it**, with four of five
  preferring the direction that became the primary concept
- **Cross-functional sign-off** from design, engineering and product leadership, and a place
  on the roadmap

## Reflection

This was my first opportunity to lead a design project from end to end at McMaster-Carr. I
enjoyed the methodical nature of the process — researching the opportunity space, framing the
problem, designing a broad array of solutions, testing those ideas with customers, reviewing
with stakeholders, then refining.

My favorite part was conducting the research study with real customers. It was enlightening
to speak with them for the first time, learn about their motivations, and have them test my
prototypes — showing that we truly care about their experience. It was good to bring some of
the junior designers into those sessions so they could get experience with research
interviews too.

**If I got the chance to start this project over, I would have:**

- **Diverged more early on**, so I could work from a broader set of ideas
- **Pushed on the boundaries of the scope** and thought less about technical feasibility
- **Been more proactive** about reaching out to others in the organization to learn more
  about the business and our customers
