## Context

<LexioContext />

### Why we decided to build Your Summary

After launching Lexio in early 2020, I teamed up with one of our other designers and ran a
research study to see how new users were finding the product. One theme came back
consistently: Lexio was **"overwhelming"** and users were **"unsure of where to start."**

That was understandable. Lexio didn't have a true homepage to give users a reliable
foundation and valuable information up front. There was simply a page with links to detailed
stories, so users had to dive in and out of different stories to get the insights they needed
for their day.

When I asked those users what Lexio should show them the moment they sign in to make them
feel confident and prepared, their answers came down to two themes:

- *"I want to see how my most important metrics are doing and how they're tracking towards
  their goals."*
- *"I want to see any outliers or trends in my data that I may not know about so I can take
  action on them."*

## Reframe

The instinct when users say a product is overwhelming is to show them less. But Lexio's
problem wasn't volume — it was that everything was equally available and nothing was
prioritized. Cutting content would have turned a product with no starting point into a
product with no content.

The homepage's job wasn't to show more or less. It was to answer two questions before the
user thought to ask them: **how are the few things I care about doing, and what changed that
I don't know about yet?**

That's why the outcome was a tightly bounded page rather than a fuller one — and why the
research finding that mattered most was a number: **three.** Most users genuinely cared about
three KPIs or fewer, so three became the constraint that stopped the fix from recreating the
original problem.

### What research revealed

Testing the low-fidelity options surfaced several insights that drove the next iteration:

- The **sweet spot for the number of metrics is three.** Most users truly cared about three
  KPIs or fewer, and holding to that prevents the overwhelming feeling we set out to solve.
- Users liked seeing underlying trends below, **separated by good news vs. bad news** — and
  wanted to toggle between them.
- The terms **"bright spots" and "red flags"** resonated, and reflected how they already talk
  about trends with their teams.
- Users wanted to see **the "why" behind the numbers.**
- **Bullet points** made the content easier to scan and consume.
- Users asked for a **visual way** to see recent performance and tracking towards goal.

### Refining on the findings

- Added **driver content** telling the why behind the numbers, as bullet points
- Created an affordance for **customizing the pinned metrics**
- Added **visual indicators** for historical trend and performance to goal
- Created a **selector** to filter insights by bright spots and red flags
- Increased the visual fidelity of the UI
- Ran the designs by several of the same users plus some new ones, to validate the changes
  and find where minor tweaks were still needed

## Craft

Since this page would be Lexio's first impression for new users, it was paramount to nail the
visual design and make it a differentiated experience from other business tools:

- Worked with our illustrator to get **hand-drawn icons** for each section
- Updated the **sparklines with a hand-drawn look** to reinforce Lexio's brand
- Used **illustrations to signal the valence** of bright spots and red flags visually
- Improved the **typographic hierarchy** so the right content was emphasized
- Created a **mobile version** so users get the same information on the go

### Implementation

I paired with one of our senior front-end engineers to build the experience with all its
interactions. To work effectively together I gave him:

- Sizing and spacing specs
- Data visualization behaviors and states
- Interaction details — the pinned metrics customization modal, hover states, tooltips
- Edge cases and empty states

## Outcome

- **+50%** active users
- **+15%** user actions

## Reflection

The biggest challenge was providing a lot of valuable content up front while keeping the page
clean and concise — the very thing that had made users feel overwhelmed in the first place. I
got there by identifying the content most important to users through multiple rounds of
research, arranging it in order of importance and relevance, and applying visual design
fundamentals to create a layout that helps users focus on what matters most to them.

Looking back, one thing I would have done differently is **bring engineers into the design
process earlier.** By inviting them to the user research sessions, they would have had more
background and insight behind my design decisions. That direct experience would have
prevented some churn when I presented the new designs.

I now bring engineers along with me in my design process, which has helped us move faster
while improving the overall design — we have a larger pool of ideas.
